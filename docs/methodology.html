Methodology

Inclusion criteria and the scoring rubric

Published

August 25, 2026

Scores are analyst judgments, not measurements. They are meant to be defensible, revisable, and consistent across the Lab — a fast read on what a source is good for, not a claim of objective truth.

What gets included

A source qualifies if it delivers insight a generalist would likely miss working only in English from mainstream outlets. In practice that means four families of source:

  1. In-country reporting — journalists and outlets producing original material from inside the country, in the local language.
  2. Independent investigative outlets — including exile/diaspora newsrooms staffed by nationals of the country.
  3. Domestic expertise — in-country think tanks, research groups, pollsters, and named subject-matter experts.
  4. Authoritative official records — central banks, statistics agencies, legislatures — used for primary data and flagged as official.

State-aligned and pro-war sources are admitted only when they have analytic value as the subject’s own framing (for example, a milblogger’s frontline maps). They carry an explicit perspective label.

The seven scores

Each source is rated 1–5 on seven independent criteria. A source can be excellent on one and weak on another; that is the point of scoring them separately. The first three describe what the source delivers; the next three describe how trustworthy and usable it is; the last describes how much interpretation it adds.

Accuracy

How reliably the source’s factual claims hold up against later confirmation and independent checking.

Score Meaning
5 Track record of verified, independently corroborated reporting; corrections issued when wrong.
4 Generally reliable; occasional errors, quickly corrected.
3 Mixed; useful but requires corroboration before use.
2 Frequently unreliable or selectively framed; use only with strong corroboration.
1 Routinely false or fabricated; value is as a signal of intent, not fact.

Uniqueness

How much the source tells you that you could not readily get elsewhere.

Score Meaning
5 Original data or access available almost nowhere else.
4 Distinctive angle or reporting that materially adds to the picture.
3 Some original material atop widely available reporting.
2 Mostly aggregates or restates others’ work.
1 Purely derivative.

Timeliness

How quickly the source publishes relative to events, and how current it stays.

Score Meaning
5 Real-time or near-real-time; often first to report.
4 Same-day to a few days.
3 Weekly-ish cadence; analysis over breaking news.
2 Slow or irregular.
1 Rarely updated / largely archival.

Transparency & bias

How openly the source discloses its funding, ownership, methods, and orientation — and how easy it is to see where it is coming from.

Score Meaning
5 Funding, ownership, and methods fully disclosed; orientation explicit.
4 Mostly transparent; minor gaps.
3 Partial disclosure; some digging required.
2 Opaque funding or hidden agenda; slant must be inferred.
1 Deliberately concealed sponsorship or deceptive framing.

Verifiability

Whether the source shows its work — cites primary sources, publishes data or imagery, and lets you check its claims independently.

Score Meaning
5 Primary sources, raw data, or imagery provided; fully reproducible.
4 Sourcing shown for most claims.
3 Some sourcing; much taken on trust.
2 Assertions rarely evidenced.
1 No sourcing; unverifiable.

Accessibility

How practically usable the source is: cost, language, and ease of getting the material out.

Score Meaning
5 Free, well-organized, exportable/searchable; low language barrier or translated.
4 Free with minor friction.
3 Partial paywall, or single-language with no translation.
2 Hard paywall or difficult to navigate/extract.
1 Largely closed or inaccessible without special access.

Analytical depth

The rigor and originality of interpretation the source adds on top of raw information. Raw-data sources score low here by design — that is not a demerit, just a description.

Score Meaning
5 Rigorous, original analysis that reframes the picture.
4 Solid interpretation beyond the facts.
3 Light analysis atop reporting.
2 Mostly descriptive.
1 Raw data or straight reportage; no interpretation.

Composite

The composite is the mean of the seven scores, rounded to one decimal. It is a convenience for sorting, not a verdict — always read the components and the note. An official statistics agency can be accurate and verifiable yet low on uniqueness, timeliness, and depth; an investigative outlet can be unique and accurate yet slow. The composite treats all seven criteria as equally weighted; weight them yourself for the task at hand. Because raw datasets are penalized on depth, do not read a lower composite as “worse” — read the row.

Descriptive labels (not scored)

  • Source type — what the source is: investigative outlet, milblogger, pollster, think tank, official agency, etc.
  • Perspective — orientation: Independent, Independent (exile), State / official, Pro-war / state-aligned. This is disclosure, not a score.
  • Language — publication language(s); the Lab is deliberately not English-only.
  • Channel — Website (a formal, standalone outlet or organization) vs Social media (an individual or account published on a platform such as Telegram, Substack, or X). Filterable on every page; social accounts carry a platform tag. The distinction matters for tradecraft: an individual’s channel and an institution’s website warrant different handling even at the same score.
  • Dataset flag — marks quantitative sources (official statistics, polling series, order-of-battle databases, OSINT loss trackers). Flagged sources are gathered on the Datasets shelf in addition to their topic page.

Revision

Scores and inclusion are provisional. Outlets change ownership, get shut down, relocate, or drift; a score should move when the underlying reality does. Treat the registry as a living document.
